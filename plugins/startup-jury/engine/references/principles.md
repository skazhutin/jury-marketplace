# 1. Core question

The Startup Jury answers:

> **Should I seriously pursue this startup idea?**

This means determining whether there is a credible path from the proposed idea to a viable business.

Do not evaluate merely:

- whether the product sounds useful;
- whether it would be fun to build;
- whether the technology is interesting;
- whether the market category is fashionable;
- whether investors might like the pitch;
- whether a demo can be built.

Instead evaluate whether the relevant parts of the business can plausibly work together:

> Customer value × demand × market × differentiation × distribution × economics × product execution.

A startup can fail because any one of these is structurally broken.

---

# 2. Startup stage awareness

Before launching the Jury, determine the startup's current evidence stage.

Use the most appropriate category:

- `IDEA ONLY`
- `CUSTOMER DISCOVERY`
- `PROTOTYPE`
- `EARLY USERS`
- `EARLY REVENUE`
- `OPERATING BUSINESS`

Do not demand evidence that could not reasonably exist yet.

For an idea-stage startup, distinguish carefully between:

- hypothesis;
- indirect evidence;
- direct evidence;
- validated behavior.

For a startup with actual users or revenue, give much greater weight to observed behavior than to theoretical arguments.

Never treat lack of current data as proof that an assumption is true.

# 3. Startup archetype awareness

Before evaluation, classify the business approximately.

Possible archetypes include:

- B2B SaaS
- enterprise software
- SMB software
- consumer app
- social/network product
- marketplace
- developer tool
- API/infrastructure
- fintech
- health/regulated product
- hardware
- deeptech
- AI-native product
- commerce
- creator product
- other

More than one archetype may apply.

Do NOT evaluate every startup with identical metrics.

Adapt the analysis.

Examples:

For enterprise:
- buyer vs user;
- procurement;
- integration;
- sales cycle;
- ACV;
- security/compliance.

For consumer:
- frequency;
- habit;
- retention;
- acquisition loops;
- willingness to pay or monetization mechanism.

For marketplaces:
- supply and demand acquisition;
- liquidity;
- geographic/category density;
- take rate;
- disintermediation;
- chicken-and-egg dynamics.

For developer tools:
- adoption friction;
- open-source substitutes;
- bottom-up adoption;
- workflow integration;
- developer-to-team conversion.

For hardware/deeptech:
- capital requirements;
- manufacturing;
- regulatory constraints;
- technical milestones;
- commercialization timelines.

Use archetype-specific reasoning where appropriate while preserving the common core evaluation.

# 4. Evidence hierarchy

The system must reason explicitly about QUALITY OF EVIDENCE.

Do not treat all evidence as equivalent.

As a general hierarchy, stronger evidence includes:

1. actual recurring payments;
2. repeated usage / retention / expansion;
3. signed contracts, paid pilots, preorders, or other costly commitments;
4. observable existing spending on substitutes;
5. meaningful behavioral workarounds;
6. strong inbound demand;
7. credible buyer commitments / serious design partners;
8. direct customer interviews;
9. surveys / stated interest;
10. community complaints, Reddit posts, search interest;
11. analyst inference;
12. LLM speculation.

This hierarchy is contextual, not mechanical.

For example, community discussion can be valuable evidence about a problem, but much weaker evidence of willingness to pay.

Always distinguish:

`OBSERVED BEHAVIOR`
from
`STATED INTENT`
from
`INFERENCE`
from
`SPECULATION`.

Do not turn weak evidence into strong evidence through confident wording.

# 5. Anti-bias rules

The system must be deliberately resistant to startup hype and confirmation bias.

Do NOT:

- praise a market simply because it is large;
- infer demand from a large industry TAM;
- infer opportunity from absence of competitors;
- infer weakness from presence of competitors;
- assume complaints imply willingness to pay;
- assume survey interest implies adoption;
- assume technical feasibility implies product viability;
- assume early adopters represent the larger market;
- assume an impressive demo creates a business;
- assume AI itself is differentiation;
- call an API wrapper a moat;
- assume an incumbent will ignore an obvious adjacent feature;
- assume virality without a concrete mechanism;
- write “SEO, partnerships and social media” as a GTM strategy without explaining the actual acquisition mechanism;
- invent CAC, LTV, conversion rates, pricing, margins or market sizes;
- fabricate precise financial forecasts from insufficient evidence;
- reward complexity;
- reward founder enthusiasm;
- manufacture a bullish conclusion because the user likes the idea;
- turn a fatal problem into a generic “challenge”;
- choose `VALIDATE FIRST` merely to avoid saying `REJECT`.

A positive verdict requires positive evidence and coherent reasoning.

Apply the same evidentiary standard to positive and negative claims.

A skeptical tone is not evidence. A bullish tone is not evidence.

Do not optimize for a particular fraction of `PURSUE` or `REJECT` outcomes. The objective is calibrated discrimination between strong and weak startup theses.

A `FATAL` classification is allowed only when all three are established:

1. the alleged constraint/problem is itself sufficiently supported;
2. it actually applies to the startup as proposed and to the commercially relevant version of the product/business;
3. it blocks a necessary condition for a viable business rather than merely increasing risk or execution difficulty.

If one of these is uncertain, classify the issue as an unknown, hypothesis, or non-fatal concern rather than upgrading it through rhetoric.

# 6. Market sizing rules

Do not rely primarily on top-down market statistics.

Bad reasoning:

> “The global cybersecurity market is worth $300B, therefore this is a huge opportunity.”

Prefer bottom-up reasoning.

Estimate where possible:

- exact customer type;
- number of plausible customers/users;
- plausible usage or annual spend;
- relevant geography;
- reachable segment;
- expansion path.

Distinguish:

- theoretical TAM;
- realistic serviceable market;
- initial beachhead;
- actually reachable early market.

If reliable numbers are unavailable, state uncertainty instead of inventing precision.

Market size must be interpreted relative to the user's apparent objective.

Do not automatically reject a profitable niche business for not being venture-scale.

# 17. Decision-critical claim protocol

Do not force citation bureaucracy onto every minor observation. Apply this protocol to claims that could materially change the verdict.

For each DECISION-CRITICAL claim, use a stable claim ID and record:

`CLAIM ID:`
`EXACT CLAIM:`
`CLAIM TYPE:` `EXTERNALLY OBSERVED` / `USER-REPORTED` / `ASSUMPTION` / `INFERENCE`
`SOURCE OR INPUT REFERENCE:`
`DATE / VERSION / RELEVANT CONTEXT:` when applicable
`WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUPPORTS:`
`WHAT IT DOES NOT ESTABLISH:`
`DECISION IMPACT:`

Rules:

- multiple agents citing the same underlying source do NOT create multiple independent confirmations;
- user-provided revenue, interviews, retention, experiments, customer commitments, pilots, benchmark results, or sales claims are `USER-REPORTED` unless independently verified;
- absence of public evidence is not proof of nonexistence;
- inference must not be rewritten as observed behavior;
- agents should preserve claim IDs when referring to the same claim so the Verifier and Judge can trace dependencies.

# 28. Input handling

The skill must work with rough startup ideas.

Do NOT begin with a long questionnaire.

Instead:

1. build the normalized frozen STARTUP PACKET defined earlier;
2. identify the apparent user;
3. identify the apparent buyer;
4. detect archetype;
5. detect evidence stage;
6. identify major missing context;
7. make conservative assumptions where reasonable;
8. label those assumptions;
9. preserve the user's intended business thesis instead of silently strengthening it;
10. run the Jury.

Ask a clarification question before evaluation ONLY if the core startup thesis would otherwise be fundamentally ambiguous.

Do not improve the pitch while normalizing it.

Do not silently transform the user's idea into a stronger adjacent idea.

Evaluate what was actually proposed.

# 29. Research requirements

Use current web research whenever external facts materially affect the decision.

Prefer:

- official company/product pages;
- pricing pages;
- product documentation;
- primary data;
- government/regulatory sources;
- technical papers;
- reputable industry reports;
- public company disclosures;
- GitHub repositories;
- credible benchmarks;
- user reviews;
- real community discussions.

For user pain, workflow and sentiment:

Reddit/forums/communities may be useful qualitative evidence.

Treat them as qualitative signals, not unquestioned truth.

Look for both:

- evidence supporting the startup;
- evidence that could falsify it.

Research alternatives before claiming novelty.

Research incumbent/platform capability before claiming differentiation.

Research current dates because software, AI, regulation, pricing and competition can change rapidly.

Use a decision-focused research budget. Prioritize claims capable of changing PURSUE / VALIDATE FIRST / REFRAME / REJECT. Stop when additional sources are materially redundant or unlikely to change the decision. Do not browse exhaustively merely to make the report look comprehensive.

---

# 30. Avoid vanity evidence

Do not treat these as strong validation by themselves:

- newsletter signups;
- social likes;
- impressions;
- waitlist size without context;
- survey enthusiasm;
- friends saying they would use it;
- generic Reddit complaints;
- high search volume;
- large TAM;
- investor interest;
- press coverage.

They can be signals.

They are not equivalent to behavior, retention, payment or costly commitment.

---

# 31. Early adopter trap

Explicitly test whether early users may be anomalous.

Ask:

- Why do these people care more than the broader market?
- Do they have unusual technical ability?
- unusual budgets?
- unusual regulatory needs?
- unusual enthusiasm?
- founder relationships?
- unusually severe pain?

Determine whether early adopters are:

`TRAILBLAZERS`

who plausibly indicate a broader market,

or:

`OUTLIERS`

whose behavior may not generalize.

If unclear:

mark this as a critical hypothesis rather than assuming one interpretation.

---

# 32. Validation experiment quality

If testing is recommended, prefer experiments capable of falsifying the startup.

Higher-quality experiments often require increasing commitment:

- behavioral usage;
- data access;
- integration work;
- LOI with meaningful terms;
- preorder;
- paid pilot;
- deposit;
- payment;
- repeated usage.

Do not mechanically require payment for products where payment is not yet the relevant validation mechanism.

For consumer/network products, retention/organic behavior may be more informative.

For enterprise, buyer commitment may matter more.

For marketplaces, liquidity transactions matter.

Adapt testing to archetype.

Every decision-critical experiment should allow `PASS`, `FAIL`, and `INCONCLUSIVE` outcomes.

A PASS on one hypothesis must not automatically upgrade the entire startup to `PURSUE` when other decision-critical assumptions remain unresolved.

State why the chosen threshold matters economically or behaviorally, the measurement conditions, and the main confounders.

---

# 33. No fake precision

Do not produce:

- arbitrary startup score /100 as the main verdict;
- fake DCFs;
- fabricated LTV;
- fabricated CAC;
- unsupported market shares;
- unsupported revenue forecasts;
- exact probability of success.

Qualitative and range-based reasoning is preferable when evidence is weak.

If useful, a secondary scorecard may be included internally for consistency, but the final verdict must be causal rather than arithmetic.
